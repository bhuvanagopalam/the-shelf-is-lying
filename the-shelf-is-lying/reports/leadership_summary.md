# Leadership summary: back-to-campus availability recovery

## Decision

Use the weekly inventory labor budget to investigate products with the greatest expected recoverable margin per labor hour, rather than inspecting the largest recorded quantities or treating every cancellation equally.

## What the analysis found

The inventory record can remain positive while units are waiting in returns, moved into clearance, stored in the backroom, damaged, or missing. These are different operational problems and should not receive the same response.

Returns matter because book inventory becomes available before every unit reaches a sellable location. Clearance matters because each store develops a different assortment and products can move away from the location expected by guests and fulfillment teams.

On the chronological model holdout, 86.7% of the highest-risk 20% of audited records were true practical-availability gaps. The clear business-rule baseline reached 80.7%.

Under the same simulated labor budget, the value-ranked policy recovered approximately $22,083 in gross margin. A largest-recorded-quantity policy recovered approximately $21,426, while cancellation-first recovered approximately $9,135. Cancellation-first found more availability problems, but many had little remaining demand or margin to recover.

## Recommended pilot

Run a four-week pilot in a small group of college-town and suburban stores during the first clearance stage of a seasonal collection.

Provide each store with a short weekly list containing:

- the product and recorded quantity;
- the likely hidden location;
- the evidence behind the concern;
- the recommended action;
- estimated labor time;
- the outcome to record after the check.

Track verified quantity, actual location, time spent, units returned to a selling location, subsequent pickup failures, sell-through, and recovered margin.

## Guardrails

- Do not assume a different clearance assortment is a failure; compare stores with relevant peers.
- Temporarily suppress digital availability only when repeated failures justify it.
- Do not spend labor on low-value problems near the end of the season.
- Treat the current results as a simulation, not a claimed forecast for a real retailer.
